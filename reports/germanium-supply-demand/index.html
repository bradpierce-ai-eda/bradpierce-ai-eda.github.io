Germanium: Why Demand Is Rising and Supply Is Tight

A strategically important material is caught between growing high-technology demand, concentrated Chinese production, export controls, and the unusual economics of byproduct recovery.

14 October 2026 · Based on publicly available reporting and government statistics

Germanium's shortage is primarily a supply-chain problem, not a sudden explosion in consumption. Fiber-optic networks, infrared optics, specialized electronics, and satellite solar cells all need germanium. But China dominates production, controls exports through licensing, and alternative suppliers cannot expand quickly.

Key conclusion. Germanium is a small-volume critical input. Even a modest increase in demand can cause an outsized price response when export access is uncertain and supply comes mainly as a byproduct of other industrial processes.

1. What drives demand?

Fiber-optic communications and AI infrastructure. Germanium dioxide and germanium tetrachloride are used in making optical-fiber glass, where germanium raises the refractive index. Data networking, telecom investment, and AI data-center connectivity support demand. This is one of germanium's major established end uses.[1]
Infrared optics and defense. High-purity germanium is used in infrared-transmitting lenses and windows for thermal imagers, surveillance, and some military sensing systems. Defense and industrial buyers face particular difficulty substituting away from qualified germanium optics.[1], [2]
Specialized semiconductor applications. Germanium compounds and silicon–germanium technologies have uses in radio-frequency, high-speed, and selected photonic systems. Germanium is important in these niches; it is not a major ingredient of ordinary silicon chips.[1]
Space solar cells and detectors. Germanium wafers serve as substrates for some high-efficiency multijunction solar cells used in spacecraft; high-purity germanium is also used in radiation detectors.[1]

2. Why is supply so constrained?

China's dominant position

Project Blue estimated China's share of primary germanium supply at 68.6% in 2025, as reported by Reuters in September 2026. Dominance at this scale gives Chinese export policy a powerful influence over availability and prices outside China.[2]

Export controls and their changing scope

China introduced germanium export licensing in August 2023 and imposed an outright U.S.-directed prohibition in December 2024. In November 2025, China suspended that specific prohibition until 27 November 2026, but broader licensing requirements remained. Restrictions on exports to U.S. military users or for military uses also remained in force. It is therefore inaccurate to describe the outright U.S. ban as continuously active throughout 2026.[1], [3]

Germanium is mostly a byproduct

Germanium is generally recovered from some zinc-processing streams and other germanium-bearing feedstocks, including certain coal-derived materials. More demand for germanium does not automatically induce significantly more zinc production. Recovering additional germanium requires suitable feedstock, specialized separation and refining equipment, and time to qualify consistent output. The result is a supply response that is relatively insensitive to price in the short run.[1], [2]

4. Why hasn't increased pricing quickly solved the problem?

High prices are encouraging recycling, substitution, stockpiling, and investment in alternative refining. But germanium supply cannot be expanded on the same timetable as purchasing decisions. New recovery plants require engineering, permits, capital, suitable residues or concentrates, purification capability, and customer qualification.

Reuters identified new or expanding germanium initiatives in the United States, Canada, and South Korea. S&P Global estimated that projects could raise non-China germanium refining capacity to approximately 126 tonnes by 2030, yet that would still meet only about 48% of projected demand outside China.[2]

Investors also face a strategic risk: if Chinese supply later becomes more readily available at lower prices, a newly constructed higher-cost refinery could struggle to compete. That possibility discourages investment absent long-term contracts or policy support.

5. The broader economic lesson

Germanium may represent only a small fraction of the selling price of an infrared system, satellite assembly, or network component. Yet the absence of a qualified lens, wafer, or optical-fiber material can delay the entire product. Buyers may therefore tolerate a very high input price to protect delivery of a much more valuable finished system.

Some applications can substitute other optical materials or redesign around different components. But substitution can require substantial engineering, testing, and qualification, particularly in safety-sensitive or military systems.[2]

Bottom line. The extraordinary price response is better explained by constrained and politically exposed supply than by unusually fast underlying demand growth. Germanium shows how control of a narrow, difficult-to-replace industrial input can create substantial economic and strategic leverage.
